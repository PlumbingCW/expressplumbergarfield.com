Water heater repair in Garfield starts with the symptom.

People rarely call because a part failed. They call because the shower is cold, the water smells, the tank is banging or a puddle has spread across the basement floor. We start from what you see and work backward to the cause.

That approach matters in a city of older two and three family houses, where one failing heater can affect several households. Garfield homes along Midland Avenue and Passaic Street often have heaters tucked beside boilers, so access and venting get checked on every visit. According to the State Board of Examiners of Master Plumbers, water heater repair must be done by a licensed master plumber or someone working under one, so ask to see a license before you agree to a price.

What the heater is telling you.

Symptoms we see most often.

No hot water.

On an electric tank the culprits are usually a heating element, a thermostat or a tripped high limit switch. On a gas tank we look at the pilot, thermocouple, igniter and gas valve.

Lukewarm or runs out fast.

A broken dip tube, a failed lower element or sediment on the tank floor can leave you with half a tank of useful water.

Popping, rumbling or banging.

Scale hardens at the bottom of the tank and water boils under it. Flushing can help if the tank is sound.

Rust colored or smelly water.

Rust points at a worn anode rod or a corroding tank. A rotten egg smell is often a reaction between the anode and bacteria in warm water.

Water around the base.

The source decides everything: a loose fitting is a fix, a rusted tank body is a replacement.

Pilot will not stay lit.

A weak thermocouple, a drafty flue or a dirty pilot assembly are the usual suspects.

Urgent versus can wait.

When a water heater problem is an emergency.

A leak that keeps flowing, a relief valve discharging hot water or any smell of gas should be treated as urgent. Turn off the fuel, close the cold water inlet and call the 24/7 line. A tank that is leaking into a finished basement can do thousands of dollars of damage in a single night.

If the problem is only that there is no hot water, you have a little more time. Even so, a cold house in January or a household with a newborn is a good reason to call at midnight instead of waiting for morning.

How we diagnose a tank.

A technician checks the age plate, the fuel supply, the venting and the electrical or gas controls. For electric units that means testing elements and thermostats with a meter. For gas units it means checking the flame, combustion air and flue draft.

We also look for the little things that cause big failures later: a missing expansion tank on a closed system, a corroded relief valve discharge pipe, a flue that has pulled loose or sediment piled over the burner. You get the findings in plain language, with the repair price and the replacement price side by side when both are reasonable.

Hard water, anode rods and the Garfield supply.

The city described its supply in the 2018 water quality report as a blend of its own wells and treated water purchased from the Passaic Valley Water Commission. The report flags hardness above the recommended range and notes that hardness scales pipes. Scale bakes onto elements and burners, which is why heaters in hard water areas fail sooner.

An anode rod is the sacrificial metal inside the tank that corrodes so the steel does not. In scale heavy water it can be spent in a few years. Checking it during a repair visit gives you an inexpensive way to add life to a good tank.
