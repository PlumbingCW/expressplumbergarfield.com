Tub Faucet Leak Repair in Garfield, NJ.

A tub faucet leak comes from one of three places. A drip from the spout usually means a worn stem or cartridge, water around the handle points to a loose or worn valve part, and water that appears on a ceiling or wall means a leak behind the surface. The first two are repairable with the right parts. The third is a wall emergency, and you should shut off the water.

This guide covers tub and shower valves only. If water is soaking a wall or coming through the ceiling below, close the main water shutoff and call the 24/7 emergency line at (973) 488-5410.

Spout, handle or wall.

Find out where the water really starts.

A tub faucet is more than the part you see. The spout and handle are the visible trim. Behind the wall sits a valve body, either a cartridge or a stem and seat, plus the supply pipes that feed it.

Leaks at the trim are the easy ones, because the parts are replaceable from the front. A leak behind the wall can run for a long time before it shows, so the location of the first wet spot matters. According to the State Board of Examiners of Master Plumbers, work that opens a wall to reach a valve must be done by a licensed master plumber or someone working under one, so ask to see a license before you agree to a price.

A dry test to find the source.

1.

Dry everything.

Wipe the spout, handle, wall plate and the tub edge so you can see fresh water. Place a dry paper towel under the spout.

2.

Run the tub only.

Turn the water on at the spout for a minute, then off. Check whether the towel wets at the handle or the wall.

3.

Switch to the shower.

Lift the diverter and run the shower for a minute. Look for water at the wall plate or the shower arm.

4.

Check the shutoff.

Turn the water off, wait ten minutes, then look at the spout and the room below the tub. A fresh drip with the valve closed points to the valve.

5.

Read your water meter.

With every fixture off, watch the meter. A moving dial points to a hidden leak somewhere in the house.

Write down what you saw and where. That record helps a plumber narrow the cause quickly. Photos of the stain, the handle and the meter reading add useful detail, especially if the leak comes and goes.

Stem or cartridge: what is inside your valve.

A two-handle tub valve usually has a stem with a washer and a seat that wears over time. A single-handle valve usually has a cartridge that controls both flow and temperature. Either part can wear, crack or collect mineral buildup.

Replacing the part is simple if the shutoff works. The catch is that many tub valves have no shutoff of their own, so you must close the main. If the main will not close, stop and call. Forcing an old valve is a common way to turn a drip into a flood, because old brass and corroded nuts do not forgive extra force. Our faucet repair team handles tub and shower valves.

Signs the leak is a wall emergency.

  • A stain or sag on the ceiling below the tub. Water is traveling through the floor.
  • A soft or spongy wall, loose tile or a musty smell near the tub.
  • Water at the wall plate even when the handle is fully off.
  • The water meter keeps moving with every fixture closed.
  • Sudden spraying or hissing behind the wall. Close the main shutoff and call right away.

A hidden leak can soak insulation and framing quickly. For help finding it, see leak detection. A pipe that has actually burst is a burst pipe repair call.

Local note.

Garfield two and three family houses and tub leaks.

Garfield has many older one, two and three family houses. In a stacked house, a tub leak on the second floor can show up as a stain in the kitchen or bedroom of the unit below. Tell the owner and the neighbors right away, and take photos for any insurance claim.

Opening a wall to replace a valve may need a permit. Confirm with the City of Garfield building department at 111 Outwater Lane, and ask the plumber to explain what will be opened. In New Jersey, this work belongs to a licensed master plumber or people working under one.

What a repair involves and what shapes the price.

A trim repair means removing the handle and escutcheon, pulling the stem or cartridge and fitting a matching part. A repair that needs wall access takes longer, because tile or drywall must be cut and later repaired. Ask whether an access panel on the other side of the wall could avoid cutting the tile.

Cost depends on the valve type, parts availability, wall access and whether the shutoff works. Ask for a written price before work starts. We serve Lodi and Saddle Brook too.
