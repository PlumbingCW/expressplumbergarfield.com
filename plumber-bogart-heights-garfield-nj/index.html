The area.

Who Bogart Heights is named for.

The City of Garfield's history page credits Gilbert D. Bogart as the founder of modern day Garfield. It says the lower part of the Harrison Avenue area became known as Bogart Heights. That is the only boundary clue the city gives, so we describe the area by Harrison Avenue and not by exact blocks.

Bogart Avenue and Harrison Avenue both appear on the city's street index. No source says Bogart Avenue lies inside Bogart Heights, so treat it as a likely reference point and not as proof. Give a house number and a cross street when you call, and we will place the address from there.

Water and cellars.

The river junction and your cellar.

Garfield sits where the Saddle River meets the Passaic. National Weather Service records for the Saddle River gauge at Lodi show crests of 13.94 feet in September 1999 and 13.5 feet in August 2011, against a flood stage of 6 feet. That gauge is in Lodi and not in Bogart Heights, and we found no source describing flooding in this section.

Treat the records as a reason to look at your own address and not as a statement about your street. Check the FEMA flood map, ask your insurer whether a policy covers sump pump failure or sewer backup, and keep valuables off the cellar floor.

If your cellar takes on water, the pump, the pit and the discharge route all matter. See sump pump service for repairs and replacements, and basement waterproofing for longer term options.

Urgent signs.

Cellar and drain signs worth a call.

  • A sump pump that runs constantly or never starts when the pit fills. See sump pump service.
  • Water at a floor drain after a heavy rain. See sewer line cleaning.
  • A damp, musty cellar with no visible leak. See basement waterproofing.
  • Several fixtures draining slowly at once. See drain cleaning.
  • A sewer smell that does not clear when you run water in unused drains.

Permits, inspections and licensing.

Some plumbing work needs a permit and an inspection. The City of Garfield building department is at 111 Outwater Lane and lists plumbing inspections on Mondays and Wednesdays in the late morning. Call the department before you agree to a job that moves pipes, replaces a water heater or opens a wall.

According to the State Board of Examiners of Master Plumbers, plumbing work in New Jersey belongs to a licensed master plumber or people working under one. Ask any contractor for a license number and look it up with the New Jersey Division of Consumer Affairs before you pay a deposit.

How to read a plumbing estimate.

A good estimate names the work, the materials and the permit. Ask who pulls the permit, what happens if the plumber finds a second problem, and how long the work is warrantied. A price that arrives by phone with no scope is a rough guess and not an estimate.

Compare scope before you compare price. One quote may include cutting and patching drywall while another leaves it to you. Ask what is and is not included, and get any change in writing before the work continues.

Before the plumber arrives.

1.

Stop the water.

Close the nearest valve or the main, then open a faucet to drain the line.

2.

Protect the area.

Move stored items and rugs away from wet floors. Keep electric devices out of standing water.

3.

Document it.

Take dated photos before anything is moved. An insurer may ask for them.

4.

Write down the details.

Note which fixtures are affected, when it began and what you already tried.

Local note.

Ask about the discharge route.

A sump pump moves water out of the pit, but the discharge has to go somewhere that is allowed. Ask the building department at 111 Outwater Lane about the rules for your lot before you add or relocate a pump.
