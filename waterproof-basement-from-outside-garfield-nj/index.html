Waterproofing a Basement From the Outside in Garfield, NJ.

Waterproofing a basement from the outside means digging down along the foundation wall, cleaning it, sealing it with a membrane, adding drainage at the footing and backfilling. It stops water before it touches the wall, which is why it is the most thorough option and also the most disruptive. For many houses, fixing gutters and grading first, or an interior drain with a sump pump, solves the problem for less.

The right choice depends on where the water enters and how the wall is built. Our team can look at your cellar and tell you honestly which path fits. For urgent flooding, the 24/7 emergency line is open.

The short version.

What exterior waterproofing actually is.

Think of it as giving the outside of the wall a raincoat. The soil is excavated to the footing, the wall is cleaned and repaired, and a waterproof membrane is applied from the footing up to near grade. A drain pipe is then set in gravel at the footing so water has a path to follow.

Because the work happens outside, the wall itself is protected from constant wetness and from the freezing and thawing that crack masonry. That is the main advantage over interior systems. Interior systems manage water after it gets in, while exterior work tries to stop it from getting in. According to National Weather Service records for the Saddle River at Lodi, the gauge reached 13.94 feet in September 1999 against a flood stage of 6 feet, so exterior work near the rivers should plan for a high water table.

The process.

The sequence on an exterior job.

1.

Check the cause first.

We look at gutters, downspouts, grading, window wells and wall cracks to learn how water reaches the wall.

2.

Excavate along the foundation.

A crew digs a trench to the footing. Depth, access and what sits above the trench shape the effort.

3.

Clean, repair and seal.

The wall is cleaned and cracks are patched. A membrane or coating goes on from the footing up.

4.

Install drainage.

A perforated drain pipe goes in gravel at the footing, with a dimple board or similar layer against the wall to guide water down.

5.

Backfill and regrade.

The trench is filled in, compacted and sloped away from the house. Plantings, patios and walkways are restored.

Ask who restores the landscaping and the walkways. That line item is easy to forget and expensive to discover later.

Exterior or interior waterproofing.

Neither one is always right. Many Garfield lots are narrow and built close together, which can make a deep trench impractical on one or both sides of the house. In those cases interior drainage often does the job with less disruption.

Exterior work.

  • Stops water before it reaches the wall.
  • Protects the masonry from constant moisture.
  • Needs full excavation, so it is slower and costs more.
  • Hard to do where a house sits tight to a neighbor or a driveway.

Interior systems.

  • Perimeter drain channel plus a sump pump.
  • Works from inside with no yard excavation.
  • Manages water that gets in instead of stopping it outside.
  • Needs a pump and power, so a backup matters.

Cheaper steps first.

Fix these before you pay for excavation.

  • Clean the gutters and make sure every downspout carries water several feet away from the wall.
  • Check the grading. Soil next to the foundation should slope away, not toward it.
  • Look at window wells. A clogged well fills like a bucket and pours into the cellar.
  • Note where water appears. A leak at the floor and wall joint points to a different cure than a leak through the middle of a wall.
  • Look for a pattern. Water that appears after every rain is a different problem than water that appears after a flood.
Garfield note.

Why river proximity matters for a Garfield wall.

Garfield sits along the Passaic River, and the Saddle River marks the boundary with Wallington. The National Weather Service gauge on the Saddle River at Lodi shows a flood stage of 6 feet, with crests of 13.94 feet in September 1999 and 13.5 feet in August 2011. A cellar on a low block near either river may face high groundwater, which no wall coating can fully stop on its own, so ask any contractor how the plan handles it.

Many Garfield houses are older, and their foundations can vary from house to house. Have a plumber or contractor look at the wall itself before anyone quotes. Then confirm any permit with the building department at 111 Outwater Lane.

How exterior work pairs with a sump system.

Exterior waterproofing and a sump pump are not rivals. A footing drain installed outside needs somewhere to send its water, which can be a sump pit, a storm connection that the town allows or a daylight outlet on a slope. If the lot is flat, the pit and pump are often part of the exterior plan.

Who can do the work.

Drain and sump piping falls under plumbing, and New Jersey requires a licensed master plumber or people working under one. Excavation and masonry sealing may involve other trades, so ask who is responsible for each part. Check any plumber through the Division of Consumer Affairs, and ask for a written price before work starts.
