How to Keep Drains From Clogging: Garfield, NJ Homeowner Guide.

To keep drains from clogging, stop the usual culprits at the fixture. Wipe grease into the trash instead of the sink, catch hair with a strainer, flush only toilet paper and run hot water after heavy use. Most household clogs are built slowly from small daily habits, so small daily habits can prevent them.

This guide covers what you do at the sink, the tub and the toilet. A repeating clog in several fixtures at once points deeper, and a plumber should look at it. For a backup that will not clear, our 24/7 emergency line is open.

The short version.

Why fixture drains clog in the first place.

Most clogs are a mix of sticky and stringy material. Grease sticks to the pipe wall, food bits and soap scum collect on it, and hair knots it all together. Over weeks the opening shrinks until water slows to a crawl.

A kitchen sink usually clogs with grease and food. A bathroom sink or tub usually clogs with hair and soap. A toilet clogs when something that should not be there goes down, or when too much paper goes at once. According to the City of Garfield's 2018 water quality report, hardness is above the recommended range, so scale can add to the grease and soap that coat kitchen and bath drain lines.

Kitchen.

Kitchen habits that protect the drain.

  • Scrape plates into the trash before they reach the sink, and let a basket strainer catch what remains.
  • Pour cooled grease into a can or jar, then put it in the trash. Do not pour it down the drain, even with hot water behind it.
  • Skip coffee grounds, eggshells and rice. They settle in the trap and pack together.
  • Run cold water with the disposal and let it run for a few seconds after you shut it off.
  • Wipe pans with a paper towel before washing, especially after bacon, sausage or anything oily.

A disposal is not a trash can. Fibrous food like celery, corn husks and potato peels wraps around the blades and clogs the line, so see our garbage disposal page if yours hums or jams.

Bath and shower.

Bathroom habits that stop hair and soap buildup.

Use a hair catcher.

A cheap strainer in the tub and shower stops most hair before it reaches the pipe. Empty it after every shower.

Pull the stopper monthly.

Bathroom sink stoppers collect hair and gunk below the rim. A quick clean with a small brush or a plastic drain tool clears it.

Switch to liquid soap if scum builds.

Bar soap leaves residue that combines with hair. Liquid soap and a rinse help.

Keep floss, cotton swabs and razors out.

They look small but catch on rough pipe walls and gather everything else.

What can and cannot go down each drain.

When you are unsure, the trash can is the safer answer. A drain takes water and the things dissolved in it, and not much else.

Does a hot water flush really help.

Hot water from the tap helps move soap and light grease along, especially if you run it for a minute after a sink full of dishes. It does not melt a solid grease plug, and it can soften some older plastic traps if the water is boiling. Use the hottest tap water, not a kettle poured straight into the drain.

A monthly flush of hot water with a squirt of dish soap is a gentle routine. Use it as upkeep, not as a cure for a clog that is already standing in the sink. Skip chemical drain openers, since they can harm old pipes and are hard on anyone who has to work on the line later.

When it slows.

What to do when a fixture slows down.

1.

Stop adding water.

Do not keep running the tap or flushing. Extra water in a blocked drain only spills out somewhere else.

2.

Try a plunger.

Use the right plunger. A cup plunger fits sinks and tubs, while a flange plunger fits toilets. Seal the opening and push with steady, firm strokes.

3.

Clean the trap.

Put a bucket under the curved pipe below the sink, loosen the nuts by hand and clear the gunk. Reassemble and run water to check for leaks.

4.

Know when to stop.

If more than one fixture is slow, or water gurgles in a toilet when you run the washer, call for help. That points to the main line.

Garfield note.

Hard water and older pipe in Garfield homes.

The City of Garfield's 2018 water quality report marks hardness as above the recommended range and says hardness scales pipes. Scale roughens the inside of a pipe, and a rough wall catches soap and hair faster than a smooth one. If you see white crust on your faucets, test your tap and treat slow drains early.

Garfield also has many older houses, so the pipes under your sink may not be the pipes you would pick today. Ask your plumber to look at old, narrow or heavily scaled sections during any visit. Mention the neighborhood too, whether Belmont, Bogart Heights or Plauderville, since a plumber who knows the area can often spot patterns.

When fixture habits are not enough.

Good habits keep the pipes under your sinks clear, but they cannot fix a problem deeper in the system. If you clean the trap, use the plunger and the clog returns within days, a cable or a camera visit is the next step.
