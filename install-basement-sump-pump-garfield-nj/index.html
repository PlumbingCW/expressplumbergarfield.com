Installing a Basement Sump Pump in Garfield, NJ: A Homeowner Guide.

Installing a basement sump pump means cutting a pit into the slab, setting a pump in it, adding a check valve and running a discharge pipe to a spot where the water can safely drain away. A battery backup is the part most people skip and later regret. The pit and the discharge route decide most of the work and most of the cost.

A small pump swap can be a one-visit job, while a first-time pit takes a day or more. If water is already rising, our 24/7 emergency line is open, and the steps below will help you ask the right questions on the phone.

The short version.

Why a basement in this part of Bergen County needs a pump.

A sump pump does one job. Water collects in a pit below the floor, a float switch senses the level, and the pump pushes the water out of the house before it reaches the slab. Without a pit, groundwater looks for any crack, floor joint or drain it can find.

Many Garfield houses are older and sit close to the Passaic River and the Saddle River. When the ground is saturated after a long rain, the water table rises under the cellar floor. A pit gives that water a place to go that is not your laundry corner. According to National Weather Service records for the Saddle River at Lodi, the gauge reached 13.94 feet in September 1999 against a flood stage of 6 feet, so a pit and pump are worth planning for low basements near the river.

The process.

How the install runs from start to finish.

1.

Pick the low spot.

We look for where water already shows up, then place the pit at the lowest corner with room to reach it for service later.

2.

Cut and dig the pit.

The slab is cut with a saw, the concrete is broken out and the soil is dug to the depth the basin needs. Expect dust, noise and a stack of broken concrete.

3.

Set the basin and pump.

A liner goes in with gravel around it, the pump sits on a firm base inside, and the float is checked so it can move freely without touching the wall.

4.

Add the check valve and discharge line.

The valve goes in the pipe just above the pump. The line then runs through the rim joist or wall to the outside.

5.

Seal, test and label.

The slab is patched around the pit, a lid goes on, and we pour water in to watch the pump start, run and shut off cleanly.

Ask for the plan in writing before the saw comes out. It should name the pit location, the pump, the discharge route and who handles the concrete patch.

Submersible or pedestal pump.

For most finished or semi-finished cellars, a submersible is the common pick. A pedestal can make sense in an unfinished space where noise does not matter. Whichever you choose, the switch type, the pit size and the pump power matter more than the brand name on the box.

Submersible pump.

  • Sits inside the pit, out of sight.
  • Quieter, because the water muffles the motor.
  • Handles tighter pits and finished basements.
  • Harder to pull out for service.

Pedestal pump.

  • Motor sits above the pit on a stand.
  • Easier to reach and sometimes cheaper.
  • Louder and more visible in the room.
  • Needs a narrow pit and a clear overhead space.
Before the saw starts.

What to settle before the pit is cut.

  • Where the water comes in. Mark the damp patches and the wall cracks, so the pit sits near the real problem.
  • Where the discharge pipe will exit. It needs a route that does not freeze solid or spill toward your neighbor's wall.
  • Power. The pump needs its own outlet, and a ground fault protected circuit is the usual setup.
  • A battery backup. A storm that floods your cellar often takes the power out too.
  • A high water alarm. It costs little and gives you a warning before the floor does.

The check valve and the discharge route.

The check valve is a one-way gate in the discharge pipe. Without it, the water in the pipe falls back into the pit each time the pump stops, and the pump short-cycles. A good valve also keeps the pump from working harder than it has to.

The discharge line should carry water well away from the foundation, because water dumped beside the wall just returns to the pit. In winter an exposed pipe can freeze, so a short, sloped run with a clean outlet is better than a long flat one. Do not connect the sump discharge to the sanitary sewer. Ask the Garfield building department what it allows before you pick a route.

Battery backup options at a glance.

A battery only lasts as long as its charge, and it ages. Test the backup a few times a year by unplugging the main pump and pouring water into the pit.

Second DC pump on a battery.A small pump sits beside the main pump and runs on a marine-style battery.Outages in your area are short and storms are the main threat.

Garfield note.

Flood history that points back to your cellar.

The National Weather Service gauge on the Saddle River at Lodi records a flood stage of 6 feet. The river crested at 13.94 feet in September 1999 during Hurricane Floyd and at 13.5 feet in August 2011 during Hurricane Irene. If your cellar took on water in either storm, the pump you choose should be sized and backed up for that kind of event.

Garfield also sits along the Passaic River, and the Saddle River forms the boundary with Wallington. For a house on a low block near either river, ask your installer how the discharge will behave when the ground outside is already soaked. Then confirm any permit or inspection with the building department at 111 Outwater Lane.

Who does the work and what shapes the price.

Plumbing work in New Jersey has to be done by a licensed master plumber or people working under one. You can look up any plumber through the Division of Consumer Affairs. The cost depends on slab thickness, pit depth, the length of the discharge run, the pump you pick and how much concrete patching you want.

Questions.

Questions homeowners ask us.

A replacement in an existing pit can take a couple of hours. A new pit with a discharge line often takes a full day because of the slab cutting, digging and patching. Your plumber should give you a time frame along with the price.

If the pump protects a finished space or stored belongings, a backup is worth having. Heavy storms are exactly when power fails. A backup pump and a high water alarm are cheap compared with a flooded basement.

Swapping a pump in an existing pit is a reasonable weekend task if you are comfortable with basic plumbing and electrical safety. Cutting the slab, routing the discharge and wiring the outlet are better left to a licensed plumber. A mistake in those steps can flood the cellar or create a shock hazard.

It should end outside, well clear of the foundation, with a slope that carries water away. It should not tie into the sanitary sewer. Ask the Garfield building department what is allowed on your lot.

Not always, but a low lot near either river deserves a closer look at pump power, the discharge route and a backup. Ask the installer to explain how the system will handle a night of heavy rain with saturated ground. Your own cellar's wet history is a useful guide.

Pour a bucket of water into the pit every few months and watch the pump start and stop. Check the discharge outlet outside for blockages. Clean the pit of silt and debris when you see it building up.
